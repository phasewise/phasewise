Most landscape architects who start their own firm have spent years being excellent at design and project delivery. Almost none of them have spent years learning how to run a business. This checklist covers what you actually need to set up before you take your first client — legal structure, insurance, fee models, software, and the operational scaffolding that keeps a small firm from collapsing under its own growth.

## Legal Structure and Licensure

Your first decision is entity type. Most small LA firms start as an LLC because it separates personal liability from business liability without the administrative overhead of a corporation. If you're founding with a partner, a multi-member LLC with a solid operating agreement is non-negotiable — verbal agreements about equity and decision-making authority will destroy friendships and firms with equal efficiency.

In most states, a landscape architecture firm must hold a Certificate of Authorization (COA) in addition to each principal holding an individual ASLA license. Requirements vary significantly. California, for example, requires a separate firm registration with the Landscape Architects Technical Committee. Check your state board before you print business cards.

**Entity setup costs (approximate):**

| Item | Typical Cost |
|---|---|
| LLC filing fee (varies by state) | $50–$500 |
| Operating agreement (attorney-drafted) | $800–$2,000 |
| State COA / firm registration | $100–$400 |
| Federal EIN (IRS) | Free |
| Business bank account setup | $0–$100 |

Budget $2,000–$3,500 to do this correctly with an attorney. DIY LegalZoom filings often miss state-specific requirements for professional service firms.

## Insurance: What You Actually Need

Professional liability insurance (E&O) is not optional. A single construction claim can exceed your annual revenue. For a solo practitioner or two-person firm, expect to pay $3,000–$6,000 per year for a $1M per-occurrence / $2M aggregate policy. Premiums scale with project types — firms doing hardscape-heavy commercial work pay more than those doing residential planting design.

You also need general liability, typically $1M/$2M, which runs $800–$1,500 per year for a small firm. If you have employees, workers' compensation is required in every state. Some clients — particularly municipalities and institutional owners — will require you to carry umbrella coverage of $5M or more before they'll sign a contract.

### What to ask your broker

Ask specifically about tail coverage. If you switch carriers or close the firm, claims-made policies stop covering you for work you've already delivered unless you purchase an extended reporting period. A three-year tail can cost 150–200% of your annual premium. Factor that into your long-term business planning.

## Fee Structure and Financial Targets

You need to know your minimum viable billing rate before you quote your first project. The formula is straightforward:

**Minimum hourly rate = (Annual overhead + owner salary target) ÷ billable hours**

Worked example: If your overhead is $60,000/year (rent, software, insurance, marketing, admin) and you want to pay yourself $120,000, you need to recover $180,000. At a 65% utilization rate on a 2,080-hour work year, you have roughly 1,350 billable hours. That puts your floor at **$133/hour** — before profit margin.

Most solo LA principals bill between $125–$185/hour in 2026, depending on market and project type. If you're in a high cost-of-living metro doing complex urban projects, $175–$225 is defensible from day one. Don't underprice to win work. Underpriced projects drain time you can't recover.

For fixed-fee work, add a 15–20% contingency to your internal estimate before quoting. Scope creep on fixed-fee contracts is the single most common reason small firms stay small — they do more work than they're paid for, and they do it quietly because they don't want to lose the client.

## Contracts and Scope Management

Use AIA contracts as your starting point, specifically the AIA B103 (Standard Form of Agreement Between Owner and Architect for a Large or Complex Project) or the shorter B104/B105 for smaller scopes. The ASLA also publishes standard agreements. Do not write your own contract from scratch unless you have a construction law attorney reviewing it.

Your contract must define what's included and — critically — what's not. Explicitly list deliverables, number of design revisions, meeting attendance limits, and what triggers an additional services authorization. If your contract says "design development drawings" without specifying the drawing list, you will be asked for things you didn't budget.

Get a retainer before you start work. Typically 20–25% of the estimated fee. Clients who won't pay a retainer are telling you something important about how they'll behave when your invoice arrives at month three.

## Software and Technology Stack

You don't need to buy everything at once, but you do need to make decisions early because migrating data between platforms mid-project is painful. Here's a reasonable starting stack for a 1–4 person firm:

**CAD/Design:** AutoCAD LT or the full AutoCAD (subscription ~$600–$2,200/year), or Land F/X if you're doing planting-heavy work. SketchUp Pro (~$700/year) for 3D massing. Adobe Creative Cloud (~$600/year) for graphics and presentations.

**Project management and financials:** This is where most small firms underinvest. Tracking project budgets in spreadsheets works until it doesn't — and it stops working around the time you have three active projects and one employee. A platform purpose-built for design firms will track hours against project budgets, flag scope overruns before they become write-offs, and give you actual utilization data.

**Accounting:** QuickBooks Online (~$35–$100/month) integrates with most project management platforms and handles invoicing, payroll, and tax prep. Hire a bookkeeper for 4–6 hours per month from day one. It costs $300–$500/month and saves you from spending Friday afternoons reconciling bank statements.

**File storage and collaboration:** Google Workspace (~$12/user/month) or Microsoft 365 (~$22/user/month). Pick one and standardize. Shared drives with no folder structure become unusable within six months.

## Operational Systems Before You Need Them

The firms that struggle most in years two and three aren't the ones that couldn't find clients. They're the ones that found clients but had no systems to support the work. Set these up before you're busy:

**Project file structure:** Create a standard folder template — project number, client name, then subfolders for contracts, correspondence, drawings by phase, submittals, photos, and closeout. Apply it to every project from day one. When you hire someone, they should be able to find anything in 60 seconds.

**Timesheet discipline:** Bill weekly, track daily. If you're not logging time the day you work it, you're losing money. Time tracking is also how you build the historical data to estimate future projects accurately. Without it, you're guessing on every fee proposal.

**Invoice cadence:** Invoice on the first of every month, net 30. Do not let invoices pile up. Accounts receivable older than 90 days have roughly a 50% collection rate. Set a policy that work pauses if an invoice goes 45 days past due, and put that policy in your contract.

## Common Mistakes Firms Make at Startup

**1. Underestimating overhead.** Most first-time principals budget their salary and software, then forget liability insurance, accounting, professional development, marketing, and the 2–3 months of slow billing that happen every year. Add 30% to your overhead estimate.

**2. Taking any client who calls.** Residential clients who found you on Instagram and want a "quick backyard project" for a fixed $2,500 fee will consume more time than a $25,000 commercial planting plan. Know your client type before you say yes.

**3. Skipping the operating agreement.** If you have a co-founder, what happens if one of you wants to leave in year three? Who buys out whom, and at what valuation? An operating agreement written after a conflict is worth nothing.

**4. Pricing based on what competitors charge, not what you cost.** Competitor rates are a market reference, not a business model. Your overhead structure is yours. Price from your costs up, then check against the market.

**5. Not tracking utilization from month one.** If you don't know your billable percentage, you can't diagnose revenue problems. A firm billing at 50% utilization when it needs 65% will run out of cash in ways that feel mysterious until you run the numbers.

**6. Deferring the project management system.** "We'll set it up once we have more projects" is how firms end up managing six projects in a spreadsheet that nobody trusts. The system is harder to implement when you're busy. Build it into the startup phase.
